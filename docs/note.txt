Low-rank models achieve lower accuracy than full-rank models. Spectral consolidation occurs

Working from the actual numbers:

---

**O2.1 — Do FR and LR stabilise at the same checkpoint?**

No, and the relationship is not random — it is governed by capacity.

In vision, FR stabilises at epoch 12. LR_r128 stabilises at epoch 7 — five epochs earlier than FR. LR_r8 and LR_r16 stabilise at epochs 17–18 — later than FR. The pattern is U-shaped around FR: very low ranks take longer to stabilise because their representations keep shifting even after the model is no longer improving meaningfully, while very high ranks stabilise quickly because they have enough capacity to find a stable solution early.

In language, FR stabilises at 37.5M tokens. LR_r256 stabilises at 10–30M tokens — earlier than FR. LR_r8 and LR_r16 stabilise at 40M tokens — at the same point as FR. The same U-shape is present but compressed by the sparser checkpoint schedule.

---

**O2.2 — Is LR stabilisation exhaustion or convergence?**

In vision the answer depends critically on rank. LR_r128 is 100% convergence across all nine layers — the model has enough capacity that its representations settle into a stable solution while still close to initialisation (mean CKA-vs-init at stabilisation = 0.68). LR_r8 and LR_r16 are 100% exhaustion — representations drift to 0.13 from initialisation before step-to-step changes become small. The model has run out of representational directions to move in. LR_r32 and LR_r64 show 78–100% exhaustion, placing them on the exhaustion side of the threshold.

Vision FR itself is 78% exhaustion — the deeper layers exhaust while the shallower layers show convergence. This is the O2.4 finding, discussed below.

In language, every single run including FR is 100% exhaustion. FR stabilises at 37.5M tokens with CKA-vs-init of 0.46 — representations have moved more than halfway from their initialisation point before the model stops changing step-to-step. This is not a capacity ceiling in the same sense as LR — FR has full rank — but it means language training never reaches a stable convergence in the sense vision does. The optimisation landscape for language has many local optima, two independently trained models diverge (as we saw in Aim 1), and the training signal is noisy enough that representations keep reorganising until the learning rate decays to near zero.

---

**O2.3 — How does the stabilisation point change with rank?**

In vision: higher rank stabilises earlier. r128→ep7, r64→ep11, r32→ep15, r16→ep18, r8→ep17. The relationship is monotonic except for a slight reversal between r8 and r16, which may be noise given that both are at the extreme exhaustion end. The interpretation is straightforward — higher capacity models find their representational equilibrium faster because they have more directions available during learning and reach saturation more efficiently.

In language: the same direction holds. r256→15M tokens, r128→30M, r64→30M, r32→30–40M, r16→40M, r8→40M. The curve flattens at the lower end because the sparser token checkpoint schedule cannot resolve finer differences between r8 and r16.

---

**O2.4 — Does the mechanism differ across domains?**

Yes, in a fundamental way that reflects the difference in what optimisation achieves in each domain.

In vision, there is a clear layer gradient. Shallow layers (0–1) in FR show convergence — they find a stable feature representation early and stop moving. Deeper layers show exhaustion — they keep reorganising until capacity runs out. This makes representational sense: shallow visual features (edges, textures) are universal and stabilise quickly; deeper features (object parts, classes) are more data-dependent and require more reorganisation.

In language, every layer in every run — including full-rank FR — shows exhaustion. There is no layer that converges in the vision sense. This reflects a structural difference: language representations are more distributed across layers, there is no clean hierarchy from low-level to high-level features, and two independently trained models never settle into the same representational geometry (as the declining FR_vs_FR in Aim 1 showed). Stabilisation in language is not convergence to a solution — it is the LR schedule decaying to the point where updates are too small to register.

---

**The core finding for the paper**

The exhaustion/convergence distinction is not simply "LR exhausts, FR converges." At sufficient rank (r=128 in vision), LR models converge — they have enough capacity to behave like FR. Below that threshold, LR models exhaust. FR models in vision converge in their shallow layers and exhaust in their deep layers. In language, nothing converges — the label exhaustion describes every layer of every model including FR, which means the exhaustion mechanism is not a pathology of rank constraint but a property of language optimisation itself.

This reframes the claim: the rank constraint does not simply impose exhaustion — it lowers the rank threshold below which exhaustion occurs. In vision that threshold is somewhere between r=64 and r=128. In language the threshold is above r=256, meaning the rank constraint is effectively always severe for the model size and token budget used here.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Together, our six aims support a coherent mechanistic interpretation of
low-rank pretraining in language models. We synthesise that interpretation,
examine its theoretical implications, and discuss what the domain differences
reveal. Limitations of this work are discussed in \S\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{A Two-Phase Mechanistic Account}

The central question motivating this paper is not whether low-rank pretraining
hurts performance --- that is well established --- but \emph{why} the damage
is so difficult to prevent or repair. Our results point to three interdependent
causes operating at different stages of training.

\paragraph{The split is structural, not learned.}
Figure~\ref{fig:aim1_language} shows that the representational divergence
between FR and LR language models is present at the very first checkpoint
(8K tokens), before substantial learning has occurred. The CKA gap changes
by less than $0.001$ between the first and second checkpoints, making a
purely transient initialisation-only effect unlikely. Figure~\ref{fig:aim4_intervention}
provides evidence for what drives this: the rank constraint imposes a
low-entropy singular value distribution on LR weight matrices at
initialisation, and correcting that geometry at $t^*$ via alpha-flattening
closes 288\% of the CKA gap in language and reduces perplexity by 62\%.
This single-point intervention outperforms all training-phase corrections
tested, supporting the hypothesis that spectral geometry at initialisation
is a major contributor to the early split. Vision results in
Appendix~\ref{app:aim1_vision} replicate this pattern at epoch~0.

\paragraph{Gradient starvation sustains the split.}
Even when spectral geometry is corrected at initialisation, alignment gains
are not sustained. Figure~\ref{fig:aim3_ratio} shows that LR models operate
in a persistently reduced optimisation subspace throughout training --- one
that grows relative to FR in language, as FR training continues to expand
its effective gradient dimensionality while LR's remains bounded by the
rank constraint. Figure~\ref{fig:aim4_dsn} shows the consequence: all three
DSN modes reduce perplexity substantially relative to untreated LR, but none
improve final FR--LR CKA above the untreated LR baseline. The corrected
spectrum is overwritten by gradient flow within a few updates, and gradient
starvation persists underneath.

Together, Aims~3 and~4 point to a two-phase mechanism: \emph{spectral
geometry at initialisation drives the initial split; gradient starvation
sustains it throughout training.} Correcting either alone is insufficient.

\paragraph{Post-hoc recovery is partial.}
Figure~\ref{fig:aim6_ksweep} shows that even reinitialising all layers and
fine-tuning leaves approximately half the gap unrecovered in both domains.
Two recovery profiles emerge. In language, recovery increases monotonically
with $k$: each additional reinitialised block contributes incrementally,
matching the layer-uniform internal representation structure visible in
Figure~\ref{fig:aim1_language}. In vision, recovery is all-or-nothing:
substantial improvement appears only when all blocks are reinitialised,
matching the layer-graded profiles in Appendix~\ref{app:aim1_vision}. In
both domains, LR pretraining appears to arrive at solutions from which
FR-like representations are not fully reachable via gradient descent within
practical budgets.

\subsection{The Dissociation of Performance and Alignment}

The most theoretically significant finding in this paper is the dissociation
in Figure~\ref{fig:aim4_dsn}: \emph{optimisation success and representational
similarity to FR are separable phenomena}.

DSN substantially improves task performance without improving representational
alignment with FR. Figure~\ref{fig:aim4_scatter} makes this visible directly:
DSN conditions cluster in the high-performance, low-alignment region ---
better perplexity at essentially unchanged CKA relative to untreated LR.
The interventions enable the LR model to find better solutions within its
constrained optimisation capacity, but those solutions are no more similar
to the FR solution than the untreated LR baseline.

This dissociation indicates that existing evaluations of low-rank pretraining,
which are almost entirely performance-based \citep{lialin2023relora,
zhao2024galore, wei2024investigating}, may systematically underestimate the
representational cost of the rank constraint. A model can achieve low
perplexity while occupying a representational organisation substantially
different from a full-rank model. If downstream tasks rely on the structure
of learned representations --- as in transfer learning
\citep{aghajanyan2021intrinsic}, probing \citep{diehl2024stability}, and
interpretability research --- performance-based evaluations alone are
insufficient. Closing the performance gap and closing the representational
gap are distinct targets, likely requiring distinct interventions.

\subsection{What the Domain Differences Reveal}

The systematic differences between language and vision are informative about
the structure of each domain's optimisation landscape, and qualify where our
mechanistic account is most strongly supported.

\paragraph{Language pretraining has a more diffuse optimisation landscape.}
Figure~\ref{fig:aim1_language} shows that two independently trained FR
language models diverge in representation space over training, while FR
vision seeds converge (Appendix~\ref{app:aim1_vision}). Alongside the
universal exhaustion-type stabilisation in language
(Appendix~\ref{app:stabilisation}), this is consistent with the view that
language pretraining at this scale produces many locally adequate solutions
that are geometrically distant from each other. The rank constraint amplifies
a divergence that is already latent in language pretraining itself, rather
than introducing a qualitatively new failure mode. This domain difference also
means our findings are strongest as characterisations of language-specific
dynamics and should be interpreted cautiously as general claims about
transformer pretraining.

\paragraph{Gradient starvation escalates in language.}
Figure~\ref{fig:aim3_ratio} shows that the gradient eRank ratio grows
throughout language training but remains stable in vision. In language, FR
training continues to expand its effective gradient dimensionality over the
full training run, making the relative cost of the rank constraint escalate
over time. This finding may have practical implications for long-schedule
low-rank language model pretraining: the longer training continues, the
further the LR model falls behind its full-rank counterpart in gradient
diversity --- an escalating penalty rather than a fixed one.

\paragraph{Performance scaling tracks gradient dimensionality scaling.}
The performance gap $\delta_r$ in Appendix~\ref{app:perf_gap} decreases with
rank at approximately the same rate as the gradient eRank ratio in
Figure~\ref{fig:aim3_ratio}: doubling rank approximately halves both. This
parallel scaling is consistent with the view that gradient dimensionality is
an important mediator of the performance gap, and accords with the theoretical
lower bound of \citet{godey2024softmax} relating model capacity to achievable
loss in language modelling. We note that parallel scaling between two
empirical curves does not establish a causal link; both could reflect a
common underlying constraint rather than a direct relationship.

\paragraph{Synthesis.}
Taken together, our findings suggest that low-rank pretraining changes not
only the efficiency of optimisation but the geometry of the representations
that optimisation discovers. Efficient pretraining methods may therefore
need to jointly preserve gradient diversity and representational structure,
rather than targeting task performance alone. Whether this requires
architectural changes, richer initialisation strategies, or interventions
that directly maintain gradient dimensionality during training are questions
we leave to future work.

**Response to R2-W1**

The reviewer's suggested test — methods that periodically expand the update subspace, such as ReLoRA — is a reasonable starting point, but expansion is a substantially harder intervention to interpret cleanly than reduction. As GaLore's own design illustrates, dimensionality *reduction* must be done carefully (naive truncation discards exactly the directions most needed for learning); the converse problem for *expansion* is arguably harder still — there is no guarantee that periodically injecting new update directions will align with directions that improve representational alignment with FR, since the new subspace at each merge is not chosen with any reference to what FR itself is doing. An expansion method could raise gradient dimensionality while doing nothing to close the representational gap, or could close the gap by contingent luck rather than by demonstrating that dimensionality itself was the causal lever.

We test ReLoRA under this lens and find it informative but not fully conclusive on its own. Following the reviewer's suggestion, we compare ReLoRA (a rank-8 adapter merged into the base weight and reinitialized every ~5M tokens) against static LR_r8. Despite an identical per-step rank constraint, ReLoRA's gradient effective rank — after an initial dip early in training — rises steadily for the remainder of training, ending 51% above LR_r8's own final value, and its CKA alignment with FR recovers correspondingly further (56% vs. 37% of the natural FR-FR baseline). Since the only difference between the two conditions is the merge/reinitialization procedure rather than the rank itself, this demonstrates gradient dimensionality is not a fixed consequence of the factorized parameterization — it can be directly manipulated by the training procedure. However, because ReLoRA's expansion is a byproduct of its merge-and-reinit cycle rather than a controlled, isolated manipulation, it leaves open whether dimensionality itself is doing the causal work or whether periodic reinitialization is helping for some other reason (e.g., an implicit regularization or optimization-landscape effect unrelated to dimensionality per se).

To isolate the causal variable cleanly, we introduce GradTrunc: a fully unfactorized, full-rank model whose gradient is projected onto a fixed, non-adaptive rank-r subspace at every step (r=8 and r=16). Unlike ReLoRA's expansion, this is a pure reduction, and critically, the reduction is not learned or optimized in any way — the projection basis is a single random orthonormal subspace fixed at initialization and never updated, chosen with no reference to the gradient's own content or FR's behavior. This removes any ambiguity about *why* the intervention might work: there is no adaptive or oracle element that could substitute for a genuine dimensionality effect. We found that this minimal, unoptimized gradient restriction — applied to a model whose weights are never rank-constrained — is sufficient on its own to produce representational divergence from FR beyond the natural FR-vs-FR seed variability, closing 69% (r=8) and 76% (r=16) of the natural FR-FR baseline, and accounting for roughly 48% and 38% respectively of LR_r8's total representational gap from FR. Because this reduction is neither learned nor tied to any weight factorization, this result cannot be explained away as "restating the low-rank constraint" — it is a direct demonstration that gradient dimensionality, in isolation, is a causally sufficient (if partial) contributor to the representational divergence the paper attributes to gradient starvation.

**Together, ReLoRA and GradTrunc address the reviewer's concern from two complementary angles**: ReLoRA shows that expanding the update subspace (via the reviewer's own suggested mechanism) can raise gradient dimensionality and correspondingly improve representational alignment, evidence against a fixed, parameterization-locked account of gradient rank. GradTrunc shows that even a minimal, non-adaptive, unlearned reduction in gradient dimensionality alone — with no weight factorization anywhere — is sufficient to reproduce a substantial fraction of LR's representational deficit. Neither result is explicable as "just restating the low-rank constraint," since neither manipulation touches the weight parameterization at all.

**Response to R2-W2**

We address both suggested controls directly.

**True step-zero measurements.** We obtained genuine step-0 checkpoints (before any gradient update, distinct from the post-first-batch checkpoint used in the original submission) 
for FR and LR_r8. At true step-0, LR_r8's CKA with FR is 0.0364 — 26.8% of the natural FR-vs-FR seed baseline (0.1358) — confirming the split is present prior to any training whatsoever, not merely by the first recorded checkpoint.

**Spectrum-matched low-rank initialization.** To isolate whether this split reflects 
the rank constraint itself or an artifact of the default `LowRankLinear` initializer's atypical spectral scale, we constructed an alternative initialization in which each 
low-rank factor pair is set to reconstruct the exact best rank-8 approximation (top-8 truncated SVD, Eckart-Young optimal) of FR's own initial weight — the closest any rank-8 factorization can get to matching FR's starting point.

This eliminates the split entirely at true step-0: CKA with FR reaches 123.6% of the natural FR-FR baseline, statistically indistinguishable from (if not marginally exceeding) the variability between two independently-initialized FR models. This confirms the reviewer's concern is well-founded at initialization — the split *as originally measured* is substantially attributable to the default initializer's spectral properties, not to the rank constraint in isolation.

However, this advantage does not survive training, and its collapse is informative rather than merely a fade-out. By the final checkpoint (50M tokens), the spectrum-matched model's CKA with FR falls to 30.7% of the FR-FR baseline — *below* the default-initialization LR_r8's own final value (37.3%). The same pattern holds in gradient dimensionality: despite an 83.2-point gap in true gradient eRank between the two initializations at step-0 (11.9 vs. 83.8), both converge to statistically indistinguishable gradient eRank within the first few million tokens of training (e.g. 48.7 vs. 52.3 at 20M tokens) and track each other closely through the rest of training, reaching a nearly identical ratio to FR by the end (3.32x vs. 3.46x).

Together, these results show that spectrum-matched initialization does not merely fail to prevent the representational split — the split re-emerges within the first few million tokens regardless of starting point, and the spectrum-matched model ultimately diverges from FR *more* than the default-initialization model does. This rules out initialization as the origin of the effect we report: a favorable, FR-matched starting point confers no lasting protection against it. We revise our framing accordingly: rather than the split being a fixed structural property present "before meaningful learning" (our original phrasing), we now describe it as a consequence of the persistent rank-8 gradient constraint *during* training, which reliably reproduces itself within the first few million tokens irrespective of initialization — a claim our new step-0 and spectrum-matched-init results directly support, 
and one that is if anything strengthened rather than weakened by testing the reviewer's proposed controls.

**Response to R1-W3**

We directly test whether the reported FR-LR performance gap is an artifact of using FR-tuned hyperparameters for LR,
as the reviewer suggests. We swept learning rate (0.5×–3× the paper's fixed value) and low-rank initialization scale 
(0.5×–2×) for LR_r8 at a reduced token budget, then retrained the best-found configuration 
(3× learning rate, 0.5× initialization scale) at the full training budget.

**The reviewer's concern is substantially validated for raw performance.** 
The paper's original fixed hyperparameters were meaningfully suboptimal for LR_r8: tuning reduces 
final validation perplexity by 69.7% (456.6 → 138.2), consistent with the 72.5% reduction found in the
reduced-budget sweep. We report this correction transparently rather than retaining the original, 
hyperparameter-confounded comparison.

**However, a substantial performance gap to FR remains even under the best hyperparameters we found**: 
LR_r8_tuned still runs at 2.94× FR's perplexity (138.2 vs. 46.98), down from 9.72× untuned. 
Tuning narrows the gap considerably but does not close it.

**More importantly, the representational and gradient-dimensionality signatures central to our mechanistic account
are almost entirely unaffected by this tuning.** CKA alignment with FR improves only marginally
(37.3% → 42.6% of the natural FR-vs-FR seed baseline — closing just 8% of the representational gap), 
and final gradient effective rank is essentially unchanged (54.1 → 49.2, a 9% difference, 
well within the trajectory noise seen elsewhere in this project). This is a striking dissociation: a 69.7% improvement
in raw performance accompanies almost no change in either representational alignment or gradient dimensionality.

This dissociation is itself informative. If the representational divergence we attribute to gradient starvation were 
merely a downstream symptom of poor optimization under shared hyperparameters, properly tuning those hyperparameters 
should have closed both the performance gap and the representational gap together. 
Instead, they move almost independently: fixing the optimizer resolves most of the raw performance deficit while 
leaving the representational and gradient-dimensionality account of *why* LR underperforms intact. 
We take this as evidence that our mechanistic claims are not an artifact of hyperparameter sharing, 
even though we acknowledge — and now correct — that the originally reported *magnitude* of the raw performance 
gap was overstated by using FR-tuned hyperparameters for LR. This also reinforces our claim that there is a dissociation between what perplexity 
approximates for downstream task incomparision to what CKA and effective Rank measures.
